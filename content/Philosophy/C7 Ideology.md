## Introduction to Ideology
- Ideology is defined as the knowledge of ideas, science of ideas or teachings about basic understanding.
- Derived from English *idea* meaning concept or ideals.
- "*Logi*" in Greek; "*Logos*" has the meaning of knowledge.
-  In a philosophical stage, Ideology is considered as an idea which is known as the **theoretical concept**. It is only when ideas are translated into *practices & movements* that an ideology is formed.

> "One of the items that forms the **mental and physical needs** of individuals that make up a society and covers political, social, economic problems, and matters related to human history and socio-geography."
> —Encyclopedia Britannica, 2022

There are three currently studied ideologies, which are:
- Modernism
- Relativism
- Post-modernism
### Modernism
- Derived from Latin *modo* meaning current, now, not traditional.
- Began around the *18th century* with the advent of the **Enlighten Movement**.
- Emphasises on the importance of the **rational mind** in determining the truth.
- *Glorify* the success of science and scientific/empirical methods.
- Main figures:
	- Immanuel Kent (German)
	- John Locke (British)
	- David Hume (British)
- 3 important concepts:
	1. Modernity (Physical)
	2. Modernism (Thought)
	3. Modernisation (Process)
- 3 basic concepts:
	1. Logic
	2. Rationality
	3. Objectivity
	- all based on **empirical data** achieved through senses & experience

Modernism left behind *old features* found in medieval society grounded in religion, instead introducing new belief in the human ability to **create a better life**. It led to a new culture based on *rationality & scientific method*. 

This belief continued into the 20th century with the success of science and new philosophers like **Bertrand Russell** and **Karl Popper** who brought the stream of modernism and logical positivism.

The ideology also called for the **reexamination** of every aspect of existence that is "holding back progress", replacing it with new, progressive and better ways of reaching the end. It also stressed the freedom of expression, radicalism, and primitivism. The disregard for conventional expectations often meant *alienating audiences* with bizarre and unpredictable effects..
### Relativism
- Theory that rejects an **objective** truth. Instead, it assumes truth can be seen from *various angles*. 
- Translates to **accepting various points of views** as different ways to understand the reality of truth.
- For example, theory A can be accepted as truth and from another angle, theory B is acceptable as well.
### Post-Modernism
- Broad movement in the 20th century.
- Often defined *negatively* as a reaction to [[#Modernism]], though some claim the ideology is a whole new paradigm in intellectual thought. Some also see it as *another phase* of Modernism, others as a *replacement* for it.
- Opposes **logocentrism**, **rationality**, **objectivity** and **scientific methods**, with the reasoning that humans also have *emotions*, *intuition*, and *spirituality* etc. Science should have *irrational* aspects.

> For example, the exchange of theories in science is not necessarily based on rational or logical factors, but also psychological and sociological factors, and thus, is not rational.
> —Thomas Kuhn, 1962

**Lyotard** (a Postmodernist) describes Postmodernism as a condition of the present state of culture, social structure andself. He's largely concerned with the role of *narrative* in human culture and particularly on how the **roles** have changed as society has left modernity, entering a post-industrial era.
### Modernism vs Post-Modernism
According to Estee (2012), Post-Modernism differs from Modernism in that it refuses to be contained by **ethics and laws**, due to the lack of interest in *tradition* and desire to venture into conceptual art & design, freedom from rules and judgement.

## Summary
The main difference between modernism and postmodernism is that modernism is characterized by the radical break from the traditional forms of prose and verse whereas postmodernism is characterized by the self-conscious use of earlier styles and conventions.

The main characteristics of postmodern society are subjectivity, diversity, scepticism, and innovation.

Postmodernism affects views and lifestyles, which in turn affects the human being’s performance of roles and interactions within all his different social systems.
